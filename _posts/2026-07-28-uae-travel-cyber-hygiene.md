Cybersecurity controls often weaken at the exact moment travel adds unfamiliar networks, physical distraction and pressure to act quickly. In guidance published on 28 July, the UAE Cyber Security Council identified six recurring risks for people travelling abroad: online fraud, phishing, unsafe public networks, public charging ports, digital identity theft and excessive real-time sharing.

The useful defensive lesson is not that every airport connection is hostile. It is that travel changes the conditions under which ordinary security decisions are made. Controls that depend on a user stopping, checking and remembering become less reliable when a boarding call, hotel check-in or urgent payment is competing for attention.

## Build the security checklist before departure

The Council's chairman, Dr Mohammed Hamad Al Kuwaiti, advised travellers to take preventive steps before leaving the UAE. These include reviewing passwords, enabling multi-factor authentication, turning on immediate banking alerts and keeping a payment method separate from the phone.

For defenders, this is a compact model for a travel baseline. Confirm that the device and its applications are fully updated; verify that MFA is active on email, banking and other high-value accounts; and make sure alerts reach a channel the traveller can access abroad. Recovery also needs testing. A recovery code stored only on the travelling phone is not meaningful resilience if that phone is lost or inaccessible.

The separate-payment recommendation addresses concentration risk. When identity, communications, authentication and payment all depend on one device, its loss can become a cascade rather than a single inconvenience. A pre-travel check should therefore cover both access and recovery, not just patch status.

## Reduce trust in the travel environment

The warning advises against entering financial information over open networks and recommends using official banking applications. If an open airport or hotel network is unavoidable, travellers should avoid sensitive transactions, disable automatic network joining and file sharing, and use a VPN where appropriate.

Network names are not proof of ownership. A familiar-looking service set identifier can still lead users toward the wrong connection, while captive portals create another moment when a traveller may disclose more than intended. The safer operational choice is to defer sensitive work or use a known mobile connection when available.

Charging deserves the same trust calculation. The Council recommends a personal wall charger or power bank instead of an unfamiliar public data port, and says users should reject unexpected prompts to share data when connecting a cable. This is a simple boundary: power should not silently become a data relationship. Organisations issuing travel kits can make the safer choice easier by including known cables, adapters and a power bank.

## Location sharing is security-relevant data

Real-time posts can disclose that a person is away, establish a predictable itinerary or give a social engineer timely context. The Council advises against publishing travel details and live geolocation without limits. That recommendation should extend beyond public posts: calendar screenshots, boarding passes, hotel backgrounds and automatic location tags may reveal useful fragments even when no explicit location is written.

This is not an argument for disappearing online. Delayed posting and narrower audiences reduce immediacy without eliminating normal communication. For business travel, teams should also agree on which itinerary details belong in approved channels and who actually needs them.

## Recovery must start from a trusted device

If a phone is suspected of compromise or digital identity theft while abroad, the Council recommends disconnecting it from networks, changing passwords from a safe device, and notifying the relevant bank or service provider when unauthorised account access is suspected. It also advises documenting the event, preserving digital evidence and contacting competent authorities, or an embassy or consulate in more serious cases.

Those steps are easier when contact routes have been prepared in advance. Travellers should know how to reach their bank without relying on a number found in an unsolicited message, how to revoke account sessions, and where their organisation expects a security report. Security teams should provide a short, offline-accessible response card rather than assume an employee can search for instructions under pressure.

The larger lesson is procedural: travel security is a sequence spanning preparation, constrained trust and recoverable failure. The best checklist is short enough to use, backed by equipment that supports safe choices, and tested before the traveller leaves.
