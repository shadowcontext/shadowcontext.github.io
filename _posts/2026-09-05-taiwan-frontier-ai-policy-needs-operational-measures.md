Taiwan has released a national policy for cybersecurity risks associated with frontier AI, organized around immediate exposure reduction, stronger product and supply-chain security, and longer-term defensive capability. Its most useful lesson for security leaders is the sequence: act with today's controls while building the governance needed for a threat landscape that will keep changing.

## A policy built across three horizons

Taiwan's Administration for Cyber Security (ACS), part of the Ministry of Digital Affairs, announced the policy on September 4. The agency says frontier AI may lower barriers to malicious activity and accelerate vulnerability exploitation. That is the government's risk framing, not evidence that every organization has already experienced an AI-enabled attack.

The policy sets three priorities. In the short term, it calls for faster cyber defense and lower current exposure by improving existing mechanisms, deploying defensive tools that are ready now, and shortening the path from vulnerability discovery and impact assessment to remediation. The medium-term track extends governance into ICT products and supply chains, with secure-by-design principles incorporated into government procurement, product security and supplier management. The long-term track emphasizes international access to frontier models, intelligence sharing on important vulnerabilities and emerging threats, and domestic AI-enabled defense built through cooperation across government, industry and research.

ACS also says the Ministry has created a Task Force on Frontier AI Cybersecurity Risks involving relevant agencies and authorities responsible for critical-infrastructure sectors. The policy is therefore more than an isolated technology statement: it creates a coordination structure intended to monitor developments and adjust measures over time.

## Translate horizons into owned work

The three horizons should not become three waiting periods. Organizations can use them as parallel workstreams with different completion evidence.

For the short-term track, start with exposure that can be demonstrated now: internet-facing services, unsupported systems, privileged accounts without strong authentication, unsegmented administrative paths and critical vulnerabilities without a named remediation owner. Measure elapsed time from validation to a deployed, verified fix. An AI tool may help analyze or prioritize a finding, but closure still requires evidence from the running environment.

The medium-term track belongs in procurement and engineering gates. Security requirements should define supported lifetimes, authenticated updates, vulnerability-disclosure routes, component transparency and supplier response expectations before a product is accepted. Contract language is only an input. Teams should verify that delivered products expose the promised logs, controls and update mechanisms, then revisit those properties when software, ownership or suppliers change.

For the long-term track, assign responsibility for trusted model access, threat-intelligence exchange, evaluation and workforce development. These capabilities need bounded data access, approved use cases, human review and audit records. A future partnership or model subscription should not be recorded as a defensive outcome until it improves a measurable task such as validation quality, remediation time or test coverage.

## Keep AI inside the assurance loop

Frontier models can increase the volume and speed of security analysis. That makes assurance more important, not less. Findings should be reproducible, mapped to an owned asset and ranked using deployment context. Proposed fixes need code review, negative testing, compatibility checks and controlled rollout. Results that cannot be independently validated should remain hypotheses.

This matters especially in critical infrastructure, where a technically correct change can still disrupt a physical or public service. Operators should separate analysis environments from production control, restrict which systems an AI-enabled workflow can reach, and require explicit approval for consequential actions. Logs should show the input context, recommendation, reviewer and final change without unnecessarily retaining sensitive operational data.

The same discipline applies to shared threat intelligence. Faster exchange is valuable only when recipients can assess provenance, scope, confidence and relevance. Indicators and AI-generated assessments should carry timestamps and handling rules, while urgent distribution paths should be tested before a crisis.

## Measure adaptation, not adoption

The ACS announcement establishes direction but does not publish detailed control baselines, delivery dates, budgets or performance targets. Defenders should not invent precision the source does not provide. The practical response is to define local measures that reveal whether the policy's direction is changing risk.

A compact scorecard could track verified exposure removed, median time from validated finding to deployed fix, percentage of critical suppliers meeting lifecycle requirements, and coverage of tested recovery paths. AI-assisted work should be compared with a baseline for false positives, reviewer effort and successful remediation—not counted by prompts, seats or generated findings.

Review dates matter as much as target dates. ACS explicitly frames frontier AI's cybersecurity impact as evolving and says measures will be continually reviewed and adjusted. Organizations can mirror that approach by recording what evidence would trigger a control change, who decides, and when assumptions expire.

Taiwan's policy offers a credible order of operations: reduce known exposure, reshape the technology lifecycle, then expand shared and AI-enabled capability under governance. The defensive payoff will come from turning each horizon into owned, testable work rather than treating policy publication or tool adoption as proof of resilience.
