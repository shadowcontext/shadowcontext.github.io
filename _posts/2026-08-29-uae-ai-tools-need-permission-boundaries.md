The UAE Cybersecurity Council has warned residents to check unfamiliar AI tools before using them and to examine requests for camera or other device access. The useful defensive lesson is precise: an AI label does not justify trust, and a permission prompt is a security decision rather than a setup formality.

## What the UAE warning establishes

Khaleej Times reported on 28 August that the Council urged users to verify whether an AI website or service is reliable and to avoid unnecessary access to device features. The report says some AI tools may be designed to collect personal information without users’ knowledge. It also advises particular caution when a tool was discovered through an online search.

This is not a claim that every unfamiliar AI service is malicious. It is a warning that discovery, branding and apparent capability do not establish who operates a service or what access it needs. Search results can lead to websites, mobile apps and browser add-ons with very different publishers, data practices and technical privileges.

## Verify the source before granting trust

Start from an independently located official website or trusted app store listing, not an advertisement, forwarded link or download offered inside a message. Check that the publisher identity, domain and support information agree across those channels. A familiar product name or polished interface is only a claim until it resolves to a source controlled by the actual provider.

Next, minimise what crosses the boundary. A user testing a new tool should not begin with an Emirates ID, passport, financial record, medical document, confidential work file or an image that reveals more than the task requires. Redaction and synthetic sample data can establish whether a feature is useful before real information is considered.

Authentication deserves the same separation. Do not reuse a password in a newly discovered service. Where an approved tool offers a managed organisational account, defenders should prefer that route so access, offboarding and policy do not depend on an unmanaged personal identity. These are ShadowContext recommendations extending the Council’s public warning into a repeatable trust check.

## Make every permission earn its place

A camera request can be reasonable for a visual assistant; microphone access can be reasonable for voice input. The question is whether the capability is necessary for the feature being used now. If the purpose is unclear, deny the request and see whether the core service still works. Convenience is not evidence of necessity.

Permission decisions are reversible. Android’s official guidance says its privacy dashboard can show which apps accessed permissions and when, with controls for camera, microphone, calendar and other categories. Apple says iPhone users can review camera, microphone, Bluetooth and local-network access under Privacy & Security and turn access off for individual apps. Both platforms also expose indicators when the camera or microphone is in use.

That makes post-installation verification possible. After testing an AI app, review its effective permissions rather than relying on memory of the prompts. Remove access that is no longer required, uninstall tools that have no continuing purpose, and investigate sensor activity that does not match an intentional feature invocation.

## Turn individual caution into a control

Organisations should give staff a short approved-tool catalogue that identifies the correct publisher, sign-in route, permitted data classes and expected device capabilities. Without that safe path, a general warning leaves each user to solve publisher verification alone at the moment of installation.

Mobile-device management, browser controls and endpoint inventory can reinforce the catalogue where appropriate. Security teams should look for unexpected AI applications and extensions, compare granted capabilities with documented business use, and provide a simple route for users to report a suspicious listing or accidental permission grant. Reviews should include tools installed for trials, because abandoned software can retain access after curiosity has passed.

The central control is proportionate authority. Verify who supplied the tool, disclose only the minimum information, grant only the capability required for the current task, and check the resulting state. The Council’s warning turns those four steps into a practical boundary between useful experimentation and unnecessary exposure.
