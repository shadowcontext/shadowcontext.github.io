More than 100 organizations across technology, cybersecurity, finance and other sectors have signed a call for a global surge in cyber defense. The letter is broad, but its most useful idea is concrete: security progress should be judged by deployed, verified protection—not by the volume of findings or the mere availability of AI tools.

## Treat the forecast as a planning case

The [open letter](https://openai.com/collective-cyberdefense/) argues that AI-enabled cyberattacks will become more widespread and sophisticated in the coming months. That is a forecast from the signatories, not evidence that every organization faces the same immediate change in risk. Defenders should use it as a planning case and validate it against their own exposure, threat intelligence and operational constraints.

The document points to familiar weaknesses: longstanding software flaws, excessive permissions, misconfiguration, weak authentication, unpatched systems and technical debt. Its warning is therefore less about an entirely new class of failure than about automation increasing pressure on unresolved ones. The near-term defensive question is whether an organization can identify its highest-consequence weaknesses and close them faster without weakening change control.

Leaders can make that question measurable. Start with services whose loss or misuse would cause the greatest harm, assign an accountable owner, map their dependencies and give remediation a deadline. Track the interval from validated finding to deployed fix, the share of critical assets with current inventory evidence and the number of exceptions without a tested compensating control. Those measures connect urgency to work that operators can verify.

## Put verified fixes ahead of AI activity

The signatories call for capable, lower-cost models to support broad defensive coverage and frontier capabilities for harder problems. They also tell organizations to verify fixes without disrupting essential services. That qualification matters: a model can accelerate review, triage or code suggestions, but its output is not proof that a weakness has been removed from production.

An AI-assisted remediation workflow should preserve the original evidence, the affected asset and version, the proposed change, reviewer approval, deployment result and post-change test. Where patching would threaten an essential service, the letter recommends compensating controls. Defenders should record the exact boundary those controls protect, test that they block the relevant path and set an expiry date for the exception. A firewall rule proposed by a model is still only a proposal until the rule is deployed, observed and tested from the correct network position.

Procurement deserves the same discipline. The letter asks organizations to raise the security bar for what they buy, build and deploy, including AI-generated code. Buyers should ask vendors for supported-version policies, vulnerability handling, secure default configurations and evidence that high-risk fixes reach deployed products. Internally generated code should pass the same review and testing gates regardless of whether a person or model drafted it.

## Give defensive agents bounded authority

The letter asks frontier AI companies to ensure agentic identities are traceable and accountable and to invest in continuous monitoring. That principle should apply wherever an AI system can alter access, isolate a host, edit a rule or change production code.

Each defensive agent needs its own identity, narrowly scoped permissions and an owner. Logs should connect the initiating person or service, the model and policy version, retrieved evidence, requested action, approval decision and downstream result. Consequential actions should require explicit approval until testing shows that a bounded automation is reliable under realistic failure conditions. Emergency stops and credential revocation must work without disabling unrelated human accounts.

Traceability also makes evaluation possible. Teams can compare recommendations with approved actions, detect repeated unsafe proposals and determine whether an apparent success was produced by the agent or by a later manual correction. Accountability is not a label attached to a model; it is a reconstructable chain of authority and effect.

## Make collective defense auditable

The letter calls on security companies and governments to share intelligence, tested playbooks and hands-on support, particularly with under-resourced critical-infrastructure operators. It proposes outcome measures such as how many organizations are protected, how quickly attacks are contained and whether fixes work.

That emphasis should shape any program launched under the pledge. Shared guidance needs versioning, scope, assumptions and a validation method. Funding should reach implementation and independent verification, not stop at tool access. Aggregated reporting can show coverage and remediation time without exposing sensitive operational details.

The letter creates no binding standard or delivery timetable. Its value will depend on what follows: funded work, bounded automation, tested controls and evidence that risk actually fell. For defenders, that is the practical translation of collective action—make assistance deployable, make authority visible and make every claimed improvement provable.
