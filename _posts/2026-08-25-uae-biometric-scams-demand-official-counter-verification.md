A biometric check can look official while taking place in entirely the wrong trust environment. An August 24 advisory from the Embassy of India in Abu Dhabi warns Indian nationals in the UAE about unauthorized agents claiming to help with visa regularization, emergency travel documents, exit permits, Emirates ID applications, or employment.

The central defensive lesson is unusually concrete: a request for a face, eye, or fingerprint scan is not validated by the service named in the conversation. The person, place, and channel collecting it must also be authentic.

## What the embassy confirmed

The embassy says a number of Indian nationals in the UAE, particularly blue-collar workers, have fallen victim to these schemes. According to the advisory, fraudulent agents induce people to provide face and fingerprint scans and then misuse that identity material in connection with financial transactions, bank accounts, loans, or credit cards created without the person’s knowledge or consent.

That is a warning about identity abuse, not a report of an organizational compromise. The source does not quantify the number of people affected, name the agents, or describe a technical vulnerability. Those unknowns should remain unknown. The actionable fact is that the deception borrows legitimacy from real administrative needs and inserts an unauthorized intermediary into the collection step.

The lures also converge on moments when people may be under pressure: resolving immigration status, securing permission to leave, obtaining identity documents, or finding work. Urgency can make an offer of simplified assistance feel plausible. Defenders and community-support teams should therefore communicate the trusted process before someone reaches that pressured moment.

## The capture point is part of authentication

The embassy’s boundary is explicit. Where biometric scans are required for an exit permit, Emirates ID, or visa regularization, they must be provided only at official UAE government facilities or counters—not to private individuals, agents, or third parties claiming authorization. It separately states that the Indian Embassy in Abu Dhabi does not require fingerprint, face, or eye scans when issuing a passport or Emergency Certificate.

This distinction matters because biometric data is not a secret that can be replaced like a password. A face or fingerprint can help prove identity only when the verifier also controls the enrollment context, binds the sample to the right transaction, and prevents replay or substitution. A scan collected elsewhere may preserve the appearance of verification while stripping away those safeguards.

Security guidance should therefore avoid the vague instruction to “protect your biometrics.” A more useful rule is location-aware: stop when a supposed helper asks to capture biometrics outside the official facility, even if the underlying service is genuine and the caller has convincing personal details.

## Build verification into support workflows

Employers, labor-support groups, travel desks, and community organizations can turn the warning into a repeatable control. Publish the approved route for each common document or status process, link directly to the responsible authority, and make clear which steps require an in-person government counter. Staff who assist workers should verify an agent independently rather than using contact details supplied by that agent.

Scripts for help desks should separate document sharing from biometric capture. A request may begin with a passport copy or Emirates ID and later escalate to a live scan or financial credential. Staff should treat that escalation as a fresh verification event, not as continuity of an already trusted conversation. They should also avoid forwarding identity material through informal messaging groups or retaining unnecessary copies.

The embassy directs people approached by an unauthorized individual seeking biometric data or Emirates ID details to the Pravasi Bharatiya Sahayata Kendra for credential verification and assistance. Its advisory lists the toll-free number 80046342, WhatsApp or SMS at 00971543090571, and pbsk.dubai@mea.gov.in. People should take those details from the embassy’s own website rather than from a forwarded message.

## Respond as identity misuse, not only fraud

When someone reports that they supplied a scan, the response should not end with blocking the contact. Record what was shared, when, through which channel, and for which claimed service. Preserve the conversation without redistributing sensitive images, notify the relevant official authority, and seek guidance from the person’s financial providers on reviewing unfamiliar products or transactions.

For organizations supporting affected individuals, use consent-based case handling and restrict access to evidence. Biometric and identity documents gathered during assistance can create a second concentration of risk if copied into broad mailboxes or shared drives.

The advisory’s durable message is that identity proof depends on provenance. A real-looking scan, a familiar service name, and a persuasive intermediary do not establish a valid process. The official counter is not administrative friction; it is part of the security control.
