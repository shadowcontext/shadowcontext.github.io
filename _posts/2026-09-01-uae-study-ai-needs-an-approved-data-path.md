The UAE Cybersecurity Council has warned that not every AI application presented as an educational tool is safe. The immediate advice is for students and families, but the defensive lesson also belongs to schools: a study tool should not receive student information merely because it can explain a lesson or produce a polished answer.

## What the UAE warning says

Al Khaleej reported on 1 September that the Council urged users to verify an AI study tool’s source, choose applications from trusted developers and download them through official channels. It also advised reading the privacy policy and checking information against reliable sources before relying on an answer.

The warning identifies several categories that should not be entered into AI tools: personal data, passwords, sensitive files and private school information. It also calls for scrutiny of requests to reach location, camera, microphone and files. Parents are encouraged to teach children not to install unknown applications or share information before checking whether a tool is trustworthy.

These are preventive cautions, not evidence that every educational AI service is harmful. The distinction matters. The security decision is whether a particular service, publisher, data flow and set of permissions are appropriate for a particular learning task.

## Treat the learning task and the data separately

A useful study feature does not automatically justify the data used to access it. Summarising a public reading passage is materially different from uploading a marked assignment, a class list, an education record or a file containing another student’s details. Schools and families should classify the input before judging the convenience of the output.

The safest first test uses public or synthetic material. If a tool cannot be evaluated without real student information, that is a reason to pause and ask who approved the service, what it retains and how the information can be removed. Passwords should never be supplied as prompt content. A request to sign in should be reached through a verified service address, not a link embedded in an advertisement, forwarded message or generated answer.

Permissions need the same task-level test. Camera access may be understandable when a student intentionally scans a worksheet; it is not a standing entitlement for every future session. Microphone, location and file access should each match a visible feature in current use. Denying a permission and checking whether the intended study function still works is a practical way to expose unnecessary access.

## Give students an approved route

Telling students to “use trusted tools” leaves the hardest judgment with the least-supported person at the moment of installation. Schools can turn the warning into a control by publishing a short approved list that names the correct service, developer, sign-in route and permitted categories of schoolwork. The list should also state which device permissions are expected and where a learner can ask about a tool that is not listed.

That route should distinguish approval for general study from approval for protected information. A service might be acceptable for brainstorming with public material but unsuitable for student records, assessment content or confidential staff documents. Clear labels are more useful than a single broad yes-or-no decision.

Families need a similarly simple check: find the publisher independently, confirm the official listing, review requested access and begin with information that would cause no harm if retained. Children should have a known adult or school contact to consult before installing an unfamiliar app or uploading a school file.

## Verify the state after use

Approval is not finished at installation. App versions, features and permission requests can change, while a one-time classroom experiment can leave an account or device access behind. Schools should periodically review which services remain in use, whether their approved data boundaries still fit the learning purpose and whether unused access can be withdrawn.

The Council’s warning makes the core sequence straightforward: verify the publisher, limit the data, constrain permissions and check the answer’s sources. For defenders in education, the additional step is to make that sequence an institutional path. A student should not have to invent a privacy policy at the same moment they are trying to finish an assignment.
