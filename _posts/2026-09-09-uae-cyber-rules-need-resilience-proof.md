The UAE has put a sharper policy question around attacks on critical digital infrastructure: when should their severity cross into the law and language of war? For defenders, the immediate value of that question is not the label applied after an attack. It is the evidence assembled before one that essential services can withstand disruption and recover safely.

## What the UAE proposed

Al Bayan reported on 9 September that Dr Mohamed Al Kuwaiti, Head of the UAE Cyber Security Council, called for a new international framework he described as a “Cyber Geneva Convention.” According to the report, he said rules are needed to protect digital assets and define when an attack reaching a sufficiently serious level should be treated as an act of war.

The statement came during the Hili Forum session on data, drones and digital disorder in Abu Dhabi. The forum's official agenda confirms that the session took place on 8 September, while Emirates News Agency's closing report says the second day examined the security implications of data, drones and digital technologies.

This is a proposal, not an adopted convention. The published material does not define a legal threshold, assign enforcement responsibilities or announce a new compliance duty. Defenders should therefore avoid translating a high-level call into unsupported claims about what international law now requires.

Al Bayan also attributes a more operational set of priorities to Al Kuwaiti: data classification, privacy, sovereignty over cloud services, encryption rules, public-private and international partnerships, and human awareness. Those priorities matter now, regardless of whether a new international agreement emerges.

## Legal thresholds cannot replace service thresholds

Governments may need to decide when hostile digital activity becomes an armed attack. Infrastructure operators face a different clock. They must decide how much degradation a service can tolerate, which dependencies must remain available and what safe manual mode is possible before an incident occurs.

That distinction turns the policy signal into a defensive programme. Identify the digital services whose failure would interrupt safety, public access or essential operations. For each one, map identity providers, cloud regions, telecommunications links, operational technology gateways, third-party administration and data stores. A service is not resilient merely because its primary application has redundancy; hidden dependencies can still create a single point of failure.

Set operational thresholds that teams can test: maximum tolerable outage, acceptable data loss, minimum staffing, restoration order and conditions for switching to degraded operation. These are internal engineering and governance decisions, not claims about the legal severity of an attack.

## Make sovereignty and classification testable

Data classification becomes useful when it changes handling. For each class, specify where data may be stored, which administrators may reach it, what encryption and key custody apply, which logs must exist and how recovery copies are protected. Cloud sovereignty should likewise be expressed as verifiable architecture and contractual controls, not simply the location printed on a service description.

Teams should know which jurisdiction governs each critical provider, where support personnel can connect from, how emergency access is authorised and whether a regional disruption would affect both production and recovery. Encryption policy must cover key availability during isolation: protected data that cannot be decrypted during recovery is unavailable data.

Partnerships also need an operating form. Name the contacts, escalation paths, evidence-sharing rules and decision authorities that would be used under pressure. Exercise them. A memorandum or supplier clause offers little resilience if nobody can reach the right person or release the necessary telemetry during an emergency.

## Prove resilience before the debate is settled

The most useful response is a controlled recovery exercise built around loss of a shared dependency. Remove access to a cloud identity service, a management network or a primary data path in a test environment. Confirm that monitoring detects the condition, operators can enter a safe degraded mode, recovery credentials work and restored data passes integrity checks.

Record the result as evidence: actual recovery time, failed assumptions, missing owners and corrective deadlines. Report those findings to leaders in service terms rather than speculative geopolitical language.

International rules could improve restraint and accountability, but their negotiation will take time. Critical-service resilience cannot wait for consensus. The UAE call is most actionable when it prompts operators to prove which services matter, what they depend on and how they will continue when a digital boundary fails.
