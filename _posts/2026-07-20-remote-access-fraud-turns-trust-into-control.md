Some of the most damaging cyber incidents begin without a software exploit. They begin with a convincing voice, borrowed authority, and a request that appears routine.

Sharjah Police said on 5 July that it had arrested a seven-person network accused of impersonating public officials and persuading victims to install remote-access applications. According to the police account, the applications gave the fraudsters control of mobile devices and access to bank accounts. The case was referred to the Public Prosecution.

The reported investigation is a local example of a broader defensive problem: a legitimate support capability can become an attack path when a victim is manipulated into granting access. The decisive control is therefore not simply detecting malicious software. It is preventing an unverified person from becoming a trusted operator.

## The application is only part of the attack

Remote-access tools have valid uses. IT teams use them to diagnose devices, service providers use them to support customers, and employees use them to reach managed systems. Blocking every remote-support product is rarely practical.

That legitimacy is precisely what makes the social-engineering path effective. A caller does not need to deliver custom malware if they can persuade someone to install a real application, approve its permissions, reveal a one-time code, or leave a banking session visible. Security warnings may also feel less urgent when the app comes from an official store.

The trust decision happens before installation. A uniform, title, caller ID, logo, or knowledge of personal details does not independently prove who is making the request. Details used to sound credible may have come from public profiles, previous data exposure, or an earlier conversation.

The safe response is to end the interaction and contact the organization through a channel obtained independently—for example, the number printed on a bank card or published on an official government website. A genuine official can accommodate verification. Urgency is not a reason to bypass it.

## Design the process so one conversation is not enough

Organizations serving UAE residents can reduce risk without placing the entire burden on individuals.

Banks and public-facing institutions should state clearly whether their staff will ever ask a customer to install remote-control software, share a screen, transfer money, or disclose an authentication code. That policy should be consistent across call centers, branches, websites, and mobile applications. Mixed messages create room for impersonators.

High-risk actions also need controls outside the support conversation. New beneficiaries, unusual transfers, changes to recovery details, and enrollment of a new device should trigger proportionate verification. A person who is actively being coached by a fraudster may approve a prompt, so a generic “Was this you?” message is weaker than a warning that names the action and its consequence.

For managed corporate devices, security teams can inventory remote-management software, restrict installation rights, and alert when an unapproved tool appears. Approved support sessions should be initiated through a documented help-desk workflow, logged, time-limited, and attributable to a named technician.

## Respond as an account compromise, not just an unwanted app

Deleting the application is necessary, but it may not be sufficient. If a remote session exposed credentials, authentication codes, messages, or an already authenticated banking session, responders should assume that more than the device was at risk.

From a separate trusted device, contact the bank immediately, review recent transactions and beneficiaries, and follow the bank’s instructions for securing the account. Revoke active sessions where the service supports it, change exposed credentials, and check whether recovery details or security settings were altered. For a work device, notify the security team so it can preserve relevant logs and assess connected business accounts.

Preserve call details, messages, transaction references, application names, and screenshots that can be collected safely. Do not continue engaging the suspected fraudster to gather more evidence. The UAE Government’s cyber-safety guidance provides official routes for reporting cybercrime; emergencies should go through the relevant emergency channel.

## Verification is the durable control

Sharjah Police’s announcement describes an arrest, not the end of this technique. Names, scripts, applications, and caller IDs can change quickly. The durable defense is a process in which authority is verified independently and sensitive access cannot be granted through one pressured interaction.

The rule is simple enough to remember: no unexpected caller gets remote control of a device. Stop, verify through an official channel you found yourself, and treat any attempt to prevent that verification as a warning.
